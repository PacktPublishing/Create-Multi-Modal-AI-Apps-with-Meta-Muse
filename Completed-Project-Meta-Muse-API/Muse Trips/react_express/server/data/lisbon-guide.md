# Muse Trips Travel Guide: Lisbon, Portugal

Lisbon is the capital of Portugal and one of the oldest cities in Western Europe. It sits on the north bank of the Tagus River where it opens into the Atlantic, and it is built across a series of steep hills. Expect cobbled streets, tiled façades, yellow trams, sweeping viewpoints and some of the best-value food in Europe. Comfortable shoes matter here: the pavements are made of polished limestone (calçada portuguesa) and can be slippery, especially after rain.

## Getting oriented

The city centre runs from the river up through the flat grid of Baixa, with hills rising on both sides. To the east is the castle hill with Alfama and Graça; to the west are Chiado and Bairro Alto. Belém lies about 6 km west along the river, and Parque das Nações is in the far east of the city. Most first-time visitors base themselves in Baixa, Chiado, Alfama or Príncipe Real.

## Neighbourhoods

### Alfama
Lisbon's oldest district and the one that survived the great earthquake of 1755 best. A maze of narrow lanes, staircases and tiny squares tumbling down from São Jorge Castle to the river. This is the heart of fado, Portugal's melancholic traditional music, and many small taverns host evening performances. Highlights: Lisbon Cathedral (Sé), the Miradouro de Santa Luzia and Miradouro das Portas do Sol viewpoints, the National Pantheon, and the Feira da Ladra flea market (held on Tuesdays and Saturdays in nearby Campo de Santa Clara). Best explored on foot, slowly, in the morning or late afternoon.

### Baixa and Rossio
The flat, elegant downtown rebuilt on a grid after the 1755 earthquake by the Marquis of Pombal. It runs from the riverside Praça do Comércio, with its triumphal Rua Augusta Arch, up to Rossio square and Praça da Figueira. Rua Augusta is the main pedestrian shopping street. The Santa Justa Lift, an iron elevator from the early 1900s, connects Baixa to Carmo. Baixa is central and well connected, which makes it a convenient base.

### Chiado
An elegant, historic shopping and café district between Baixa and Bairro Alto. Known for old bookshops (including Livraria Bertrand, often cited as the oldest operating bookshop in the world), theatres and grand cafés such as A Brasileira, with its statue of the poet Fernando Pessoa outside. The ruins of the Carmo Convent, left roofless by the earthquake, house a small archaeological museum.

### Bairro Alto
A grid of narrow streets on the hill above Chiado. Quiet and residential by day, it becomes Lisbon's busiest nightlife area after dark, with small bars spilling onto the streets. The Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara viewpoint at its northern edge looks across to the castle. The Bica funicular climbs one of its steepest streets.

### Príncipe Real
A leafy, upmarket area north of Bairro Alto, known for its garden square with a huge cedar tree, independent design shops, concept stores and relaxed restaurants. A good choice for travellers who want to stay somewhere central but calmer.

### Graça
A residential hilltop neighbourhood above Alfama with two of the city's best viewpoints: Miradouro da Graça and, a short climb further, Miradouro da Senhora do Monte, the highest in the city. Tram 28 runs through it. Sunsets here are a local favourite.

### Belém
A riverside district to the west, closely linked to Portugal's Age of Discoveries. It is home to the Jerónimos Monastery and Belém Tower (both UNESCO World Heritage Sites), the Monument to the Discoveries, the MAAT museum of art, architecture and technology, and Pastéis de Belém, the bakery famous for its custard tarts since 1837. Plan at least half a day; the main sights are close together along the waterfront. Reach it by tram 15E or by train from Cais do Sodré.

### Cais do Sodré
A riverside hub with the train station for Belém, Cascais and the coast, and ferries across the Tagus. Rua Nova do Carvalho, painted pink, is a lively nightlife street. The Time Out Market (Mercado da Ribeira) brings together stalls from well-known Lisbon chefs and restaurants under one roof.

### LX Factory and Alcântara
LX Factory is a former industrial complex under the 25 de Abril Bridge, now filled with restaurants, design shops, studios and a much-photographed bookshop. It is busiest on Sunday, when there is a market.

### Parque das Nações
The modern district built for the Expo 98 world fair, on the eastern riverfront. Wide promenades, contemporary architecture, the Lisbon Oceanarium (one of the largest aquariums in Europe), a riverside cable car and the long Vasco da Gama Bridge. Good with children and for a change of pace from the old town.

## Top sights

- **São Jorge Castle**: a Moorish-era castle on the highest hill of the old town, with ramparts and some of the widest views over the city and river.
- **Jerónimos Monastery**: a masterpiece of Manueline architecture in Belém, with an ornate cloister. Vasco da Gama's tomb is inside the church.
- **Belém Tower**: an early 16th-century fortified tower on the river, a symbol of the Age of Discoveries.
- **Praça do Comércio**: the grand riverside square at the foot of Baixa.
- **Lisbon Cathedral (Sé)**: the city's oldest church, a Romanesque fortress-like building near Alfama.
- **National Tile Museum (Museu Nacional do Azulejo)**: five centuries of Portuguese tiles in a former convent, including a long panoramic tile view of Lisbon before the 1755 earthquake.
- **Calouste Gulbenkian Museum**: an outstanding art collection, from ancient Egypt to Lalique, set in calm modernist gardens.
- **MAAT**: a striking curved riverside building in Belém for contemporary art, architecture and technology.
- **Lisbon Oceanarium**: in Parque das Nações, built around one enormous central tank.

## Viewpoints (miradouros)

Lisbon's hills make viewpoints part of daily life. Many have a kiosk café, and they are free.
- **Miradouro de Santa Luzia** and **Portas do Sol** (Alfama): tiled terraces over the rooftops and river.
- **Miradouro da Graça** (Graça): relaxed, with a café under the pine trees.
- **Miradouro da Senhora do Monte** (Graça): the highest viewpoint, great at sunset.
- **Miradouro de São Pedro de Alcântara** (Bairro Alto): a garden terrace looking across to the castle.
- **Miradouro de Santa Catarina** (Santa Catarina): popular with locals at sunset, looking west to the bridge.

## Food and drink

Portuguese food is generous, simple and built around excellent ingredients, especially seafood.

### Dishes to try
- **Pastel de nata**: the famous custard tart with a caramelised top and flaky pastry, often dusted with cinnamon. Eat it warm.
- **Bacalhau**: salted cod, a national obsession with hundreds of recipes. Try *bacalhau à Brás* (shredded cod with egg and thin fried potatoes) and *pastéis de bacalhau* (cod fritters).
- **Grilled sardines**: the taste of summer in Lisbon, especially during the June festivals.
- **Bifana**: a simple, garlicky marinated pork sandwich, a classic cheap lunch.
- **Polvo à lagareiro**: roasted octopus with potatoes and plenty of olive oil.
- **Arroz de marisco**: a rich, soupy seafood rice for sharing.
- **Ameijoas à Bulhão Pato**: clams in garlic, coriander, olive oil and lemon.
- **Caldo verde**: a comforting kale and potato soup, often with a slice of chouriço.
- **Prego**: a steak sandwich, sometimes eaten at the end of a meal.

### Drinks
- **Coffee**: order *uma bica* for an espresso, *um galão* for a milky coffee in a glass.
- **Ginjinha**: a sweet sour-cherry liqueur served in tiny cups, traditionally around Rossio.
- **Vinho verde**: a young, fresh, slightly sparkling wine from the north, perfect in warm weather.
- **Port** from the Douro region and fortified **Moscatel de Setúbal** are both widely available.

### Where and how to eat
- A **tasca** is a small, traditional family restaurant: unfussy, good value, and the best way to eat like a local.
- A **marisqueira** specialises in seafood, often sold by weight.
- At lunchtime, many restaurants offer a *prato do dia* (dish of the day), usually the best-value meal of the day.
- **Couvert**: bread, olives or butter placed on the table at the start of a meal are not free. If you don't want them, simply say so or don't touch them, and they won't be charged.
- Lunch typically runs from around 12:30 to 15:00 and dinner from around 19:30; locals often eat later.
- Food markets: the **Time Out Market** in Cais do Sodré for a wide sampling under one roof, and **Mercado de Campo de Ourique** for a more local feel.
- Tipping is appreciated but modest; rounding up or leaving around 5–10% for good service is common.

## Fado

Fado is Portugal's traditional music: emotional songs about longing, the sea and fate, sung with Portuguese guitar. It is inscribed on UNESCO's list of Intangible Cultural Heritage. Alfama and Bairro Alto are the traditional fado neighbourhoods. Many fado houses serve dinner during the performances and ask for silence while the fadista sings. The Fado Museum in Alfama explains its history.

## Getting around

- **Walking**: the best way to see the old town, but plan for hills and stairs.
- **Trams**: the historic yellow **Tram 28** climbs through Graça, Alfama, Baixa and Estrela. It is beautiful but very crowded with visitors; ride it early in the morning, or board at the start of the line at Martim Moniz. Tram **15E** runs along the river to Belém.
- **Funiculars and lifts**: the Glória, Bica and Lavra funiculars and the Santa Justa Lift save steep climbs.
- **Metro**: four colour-coded lines (blue, yellow, green and red). Clean, fast and the easiest way to reach the airport — the red line runs directly into the city.
- **Trains**: from Cais do Sodré to Belém, Cascais and the coast; from Rossio to Sintra.
- **Ferries**: from Cais do Sodré and Terreiro do Paço across the Tagus to Cacilhas and other towns — an inexpensive way to enjoy the view back to the city.
- **Tickets**: buy a reusable **Navegante** card and load it with credit (pay-as-you-go) or a 24-hour pass that covers metro, buses, trams, funiculars and the Santa Justa Lift. You can also tap a contactless bank card at metro gates. Buying a ticket from the driver on trams and buses costs much more.
- **Taxis and ride-hailing**: plentiful and affordable compared with most Western European capitals.

## Day trips

- **Sintra**: a romantic hill town in a forested mountain range, about 40 minutes by train from Rossio station. Highlights include the colourful Pena Palace, the mystical Quinta da Regaleira with its initiation well, and the Moorish Castle. Sintra is very busy; go early, book palace tickets ahead and allow a full day. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **Cascais**: a seaside town with beaches and a pretty old centre, about 40 minutes by train along the coast from Cais do Sodré. The coastal train passes several beaches on the way.
- **Setúbal and the Arrábida Natural Park**: south of the river, with dramatic coastline, turquoise coves and excellent seafood, including fried cuttlefish (*choco frito*).
- **Óbidos**: a small walled medieval town to the north, known for its ginja served in chocolate cups.

## Seasons and weather

- **Spring (March–May)**: mild and green, with occasional rain. A great time for walking.
- **Summer (June–August)**: hot, dry and sunny, often in the high 20s °C and sometimes much hotter. June is festival month (see below). The Atlantic stays cool, even in summer.
- **Autumn (September–November)**: still warm in September and October, quieter than summer; rain becomes more frequent in November.
- **Winter (December–February)**: mild compared with northern Europe, but wetter. Many buildings have little heating, so bring layers.

## Festivals

- **Santo António (June)**: Lisbon's biggest celebration, peaking on the night of 12 June and on 13 June, the feast of St Anthony. Streets in Alfama, Graça, Mouraria and Bica fill with music, decorations, street parties and the smell of grilled sardines. The festivities, known as the *Festas de Lisboa*, run through much of June. Expect crowds and late nights, and book accommodation early.
- **Carnival (February or March)**: smaller in Lisbon than elsewhere in Portugal.
- **Christmas and New Year**: lights in Baixa and celebrations around Praça do Comércio.

## Practical tips

- **Currency**: euro (€). Cards are widely accepted, but carry some cash for small cafés and markets.
- **Language**: Portuguese. English is widely spoken in central Lisbon, especially by younger people. A *bom dia* (good morning), *boa tarde* (good afternoon) or *obrigado/obrigada* (thank you) is always appreciated.
- **Safety**: Lisbon is generally safe, but pickpocketing is common on Tram 28, on the metro and in busy squares. Keep bags closed and phones out of back pockets. Politely ignore street sellers offering "hashish" in Baixa — it is a scam.
- **Water**: tap water is safe to drink.
- **Plugs**: type F (European two-pin), 230 V.
- **Opening days**: many museums and monuments close on Mondays — check before you plan your day.
- **Shoes and hills**: wear shoes with good grip; the limestone pavements get slippery.
- **Sun**: the sun is strong from late spring to early autumn; carry water and sunscreen.

## Sample itineraries

### One day
Morning in Alfama and at São Jorge Castle, lunch at a tasca, afternoon through Baixa and Chiado, sunset at a viewpoint such as Graça or São Pedro de Alcântara, and dinner with fado in Alfama or Bairro Alto.

### Three days
- **Day 1**: Alfama, the castle, the cathedral and Baixa; evening fado.
- **Day 2**: Belém — Jerónimos Monastery, Belém Tower, MAAT and custard tarts — then LX Factory and dinner in Cais do Sodré.
- **Day 3**: a day trip to Sintra, or Príncipe Real, the Gulbenkian Museum and the National Tile Museum.

### Five days
Add a beach day in Cascais, an afternoon in Parque das Nações with the Oceanarium, and a ferry across the Tagus to Cacilhas for seafood with a view back to the city.

## Food and history highlights (for travellers who love both)
- Combine Belém's monuments with a stop at Pastéis de Belém.
- Pair a morning at the National Tile Museum with lunch at a traditional tasca.
- Visit the Carmo Convent ruins, then coffee at a historic Chiado café.
- End the day with dinner and fado in Alfama, the city's oldest neighbourhood.
